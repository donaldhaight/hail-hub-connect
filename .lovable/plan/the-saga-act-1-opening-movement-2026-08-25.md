## Introduction — A Letter Left on the Table

This is not a business plan. A business plan asks permission. This is a record of a thing already under construction, written while it is being built, by the two of us building it.

One of us has spent twenty-five years inside the insurance restoration market — the industry that shows up after the hail, after the wind, after the water. The other is a machine that reads everything it is given and forgets nothing it is told to keep. Between us there is a corpus going back to 2008: filings, decks, screenshots, dated correspondence, dead companies, live scars. Every claim in this book that can be dated, is dated. Everything else wears a label saying what it is — assertion, hypothesis, decision — so that a stranger can audit our confidence without trusting our enthusiasm.

The wager is small enough to state in one line and large enough to take a life: **if we can fix the insurance restoration market, we can fix the way this country coordinates.**

You do not have to believe the second half yet. You only have to watch the first half work.

## Chapter 1 — The Storm Is Not the Disaster

A hailstorm is a twenty-minute event. The disaster is the eighteen months that follow.

Here is what actually happens. The storm passes. Within seventy-two hours the neighborhood is a marketplace: adjusters, roofers, public adjusters, restoration crews, attorneys, lenders, and a floating population of men in trucks with out-of-state plates. Every one of them holds a piece of the truth about that house. None of them holds the whole thing. Nobody is lying. Everybody is guessing with partial information, and every guess costs somebody money.

The homeowner learns the vocabulary of their own catastrophe from a stranger who is paid by outcome. The carrier prices a roof it will never stand on. The contractor bids against a scope written by someone with a different incentive. The lender releases funds against a photograph. The result is not fraud in most cases — it is drift. Ten thousand small honest divergences, compounding, until the file closes at a number nobody can reconstruct and everybody resents.

I have been on every side of that table. I have written the scope. I have argued the supplement. I have watched a good crew go under because the money moved three weeks slower than the payroll.

The industry calls this friction, as if it were a cost of doing business. It is not friction. It is a **coordination failure**, and coordination failure is the same disease that kills federal programs, disaster response, and grant-funded science. Restoration is simply the version of it that runs fast enough to observe in a single season, and small enough that two people with a corpus can actually fix it.

That is why we start here. Not because roofs are important. Because roofs are legible.

## Chapter 2 — Leads Were Never the Product

The first version of this, in 2008, sold leads.

Leads for roofing projects after hail. Leads for salespeople — recruiting for contractors who could never keep a crew through a slow month. It worked the way lead generation always works: it worked in a big storm year and it starved otherwise, and the moment a lead was sold twice the trust it depended on was gone.

It took years to see the actual shape of what I had built. I was not selling information about roofs. I was selling **a moment of matched intent** — the instant when a party with a need and a party with capacity both existed and could not find each other. The lead was the receipt for that moment, and I was selling the receipt instead of owning the exchange.

Barry Diller understood this at a scale most people never noticed. He did not build a travel company; he bought the moment someone decides to go somewhere, then bought every adjacent moment around it — the search, the price comparison, the booking, the review, the ticket. Not one business. A **portfolio of intent capture** around a single human decision, where each property makes the others more valuable and the customer never has to leave the estate.

Apply that to a hailstorm. The homeowner's decision is not "who fixes my roof." It is a chain: is there damage, is it covered, what is it worth, who does the work, who verifies it, who pays whom and when, and who will vouch for all of it in three years when the house sells. Seven decisions. Seven different parties currently holding seven partial records.

Diller's answer would be to own each node and connect them. That is the right answer. But there is a piece he never needed and we cannot do without: in his markets the parties are strangers who transact once. In ours they are the same few hundred people in a region, transacting with each other for decades, with no shared memory of who kept their word.

Which is the actual product. Not leads. **Memory.**

## Chapter 3 — The Human Blockchain

The word blockchain has been ruined by people selling coins, so let me define it by what it does rather than what it costs.

A ledger is useful when three things are true: entries are dated and cannot be quietly changed, everyone who matters can read it, and no single party owns the writing. That is it. Nothing about that requires a token, an exchange, or a whitepaper. It requires discipline about who writes what, when, and what it means.

The Human Blockchain is that discipline applied to people instead of hashes. Seven founding stakeholder groups — the parties who already exist around every claim — each with the authority to write only what they are actually in a position to know, and no authority to overwrite anyone else. The adjuster writes the inspection. The contractor writes the scope and the completion. The verifier writes the verification. The bank writes the release. The homeowner writes the acceptance. Every entry is dated, attributed, and labeled with what kind of statement it is. Nobody has to be trusted, because nobody is being asked to vouch for anything outside their own line of sight.

And — this matters more than it sounds — **it works with the power off.** A ledger of dated human attestations, held by the parties who made them, is still a ledger on paper in a county building during a three-week blackout. Silicon accelerates it. Silicon does not constitute it. Any system that stops working when the grid does was never infrastructure; it was a convenience.

That is the civilian counterpart to what the federal government is now attempting from the top down with the Genesis Mission: coordination, provenance, and trusted-decision speed. They are building it for science, with federal money, from Washington. We are building it for restoration, with billable work, from a ranch in Flower Mound.

One of those two will be demonstrable in a room of three hundred people on **November 1, 2026**.
